## Selected methods, adapted for this pilot

- **Research before composition:** investigate what is offered, competing approaches, and what the audience actually wants. For a build newsletter, the equivalent is the real product/decision evidence, competing explanations or writing examples, and the reader's interest. Do not invent customer voice when no interviews/comments exist.
- **Awareness:** readers enter at different awareness stages. An issue for builders familiar with tooling needs different background than a cold prospect.
- **Rule of One (RIOA):** one reader, one idea, one offer and one action is a useful copy focus mechanism. Adapt for editorial: one primary reader and takeaway; a newsletter need not force a commercial offer or CTA if it would weaken the story.
- **Five reader questions:** Why relevant now? What new thing is here? What is gained? How does it actually work? Can I trust the source?
- **Messaging hierarchy and spit drafting:** decide the logical proof and the job of each passage before polishing sentences. Allow a flexible final shape.
- **Lead/body/close:** use attention and relevance to open, proof to develop, and a proportionate next action or stopping point; do not impose equal-length sections.
- **CUB editing:** test whether actual readers find passages confusing, unbelievable or boring. Reader feedback is stronger evidence than a self-generated rubric.
- **Swappy editing:** the source uses a two-part final check: "so what?" and "prove it?". Remove lines without a reader purpose or sufficient evidence.
- The source material cautions that formulas are not universal and must be adapted/tested.

## Newsletter adaptation — research before writing

**Mandatory preflight:** use [RESEARCH-WORKFLOW.md](RESEARCH-WORKFLOW.md) first. Collect a business/reader/source research pack and a responsible RIOA editorial brief before choosing a story. Do not start by rewriting a raw activity journal. The worksheets ask about guarantees and offers for sales copy; editorial issues must not invent guarantees or promises.

## Newsletter adaptation — exercise

1. Complete the product, audience, brand, competitor/editorial and voice-of-customer research stages in the linked method. Record missing evidence rather than filling imagined customer quotes.
2. Identify the publication and one reader's *current problem or curiosity*.
3. Make a source table: verified event/fact, uncertainty, allowed public disclosure, reader relevance.
4. Choose one arguable idea, not a week-in-review chronology. Produce 2–3 substantively different angles.
5. Write a proof map: what must a skeptical reader know, and which actual artifact proves each claim? Mark gaps as `[NEED]`.
6. Write the issue in the voice of the configured business, with uneven space devoted to what is genuinely specific.
7. Run the five reader questions, CUB, and Swappy. Preserve meaningful uncertainty.
8. Compare baseline and revision with the *same source materials*; do not simply reward length, a more polished tone, or invented emotional texture.

## Voice constraint — no guru stance

Writing expertise belongs to the **editor**, not to a fabricated public author persona. The newsletter narrator can say what they experienced, what the sources show, what they still do not know, and what readers might test for themselves. Do not claim to be a senior technical expert, recruiter, growth authority or proven founder unless supported. No outcome guarantees (job offers, SEO rank, revenue, growth, AI accuracy). Avoid condescending advice or universal formulas; make clear the scope of each claim without adding robotic caveats to every sentence.
