## 1. Sticky research, adapted to publishing

The original template asks for product facts, benefits and USPs; ideal client; existing brand; three competitors; market/voice-of-customer (VoC), including pains, objections, desires, beliefs and words/phrases; and synthesis/ranking.

Before an editorial assignment:
1. **Business/product truth.** What is actually offered or shipped? Differentiate roadmap and hypothesis. Where does the story have authentic first-hand evidence?
2. **Reader evidence.** What question is a defined reader asking, and what do their actual words and comments say? Record a source link, time and context. Do not infer demographics or demand from stereotypes.
3. **Existing voice/credibility.** What does the business actually have standing to explain? If it has only its own prototype, explicitly say so; no fake authority.
4. **Competitive editorial coverage.** Study 2–3 real articles about the topic, including their strengths, missing answers and quality of evidence. Do not copy their prose or invent competitor weaknesses.
5. **VoC and constraints.** Collect authentic public comments/questions, not AI-written simulated quotes. Separate sourced observations from hypotheses. Flag nonpublic anecdotes.
6. **Research synthesis.** Rank distinct topic angles by reader value, verified original evidence, under-served questions, and practical feasibility; recommend *no issue yet* when the materials are thin.

The cited course's original ideal-client template includes gender, age, income and family status. **Do not fill fields by invention or assume they are necessary**. For an editorial issue, readers' task, concerns, context and awareness are often more useful than unsupported demographic profiles.

## 2. RIOA adapted for honest editorial

The original Copy Planning Template spells out One Reader, One Idea, One Offer, One Action for direct-response copy. It asks about deliverables, guarantee, bonuses and price. Those are questions for a **sales offer**, not mandatory ingredients in a newsletter.

| Source concept | Editorial interpretation |
|---|---|
| One Reader | A concrete reader with a real question, research evidence and level of awareness |
| One Idea | A specific argument or question the issue will substantively resolve |
| One Offer | The reader benefit of **this issue**: evidence, explanation, tested process, artifact or perspective, never a promised life outcome |
| One Action | Optional, proportionate next step: inspect the artifact, test a method, reply with a question or simply reflect. No forced CTA |

No guarantee of a job, revenue, ranking, growth, virality, AI reliability or "proven formula". A result observed in one case is a case study, not a universal promise.

## 3. Outline and draft from evidence

- Summarize the question, primary idea, evidence and reader benefit before an outline.
- Plan a proof/order of information, not a rigid newsletter template.
- Show real artifacts where permitted: revised PRD, code diff, screenshots, test results, sample input/output labeled as sample.
- Give enough space to interpret how the decision was made, what was uncertain, competing options and failures. Length is earned by information, not compressed to an arbitrary 400–600 words.
- The cited source material advises attention management and argues that a short attention window does *not* require short copy. Editorial writing still should not be padded.
- Use CUB with real readers (confusing, unbelievable, boring), then "so what?" / "prove it?" on weak sections. A self-graded "human score" isn't validation.

## 4. Claim and persona integrity

The author is a practitioner sharing what they did or researched. Never write as a career guru, recruiter insider, senior engineer or proven growth expert unless explicitly supported by the author's actual credentials and remit.

Prefer clear scoped statements, e.g. "In this prototype, the second login problem appeared only after we fixed the first." Distinguish "what I observed", "what I think explains it", "what external evidence suggests", and "what I haven't tested". Avoid repetitive hedging; concise uncertainty is more credible than false certainty.

Never copy personal stories, purported statistics, testimonials, paid course pages or worksheet text into distributable skills. Preserve pointers to originals.

## 5. Research deliverables (before drafting)

A private, dated research pack must contain:
- business/source-of-truth snapshot and stage;
- audience question and real VoC links or explicit "not yet observed";
- relevant competitor/editorial analysis;
- one main insight and two alternative angles, with reasons and source links;
- evidence/claims to use and claims not permitted;
- RIOA editorial brief (no manufactured offer or guarantee);
- recommendation: proceed / research more / skip;
- editorial persona and tone limits.

**Promotion gate:** no drafting unless the topic is worth an issue and essential claims can be supported. Don't consider passing a mechanical metadata gate sufficient.
