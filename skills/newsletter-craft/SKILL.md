# Newsletter editorial craft — portable, additive skill

**Source and status:** A research-based practice guide distilled from public newsletter-specialist education and actual articles, **not** a completed professional course credential or validated quality guarantee. Read [education assessment](../../docs/research/NEWSLETTER-EDUCATION-ASSESSMENT.md) for exact source URLs, access caveats and what was inspected. Augments, does not replace, research-first copywriting skills.

## Before proposing an issue

1. **Publication promise.** Read the tenant's actual editorial programs, audience and public/private boundaries. In a sentence: why would this exact reader want a *recurring email* from this publication rather than search results or a product update?
2. **Curiosity and material.** Scan an authorized idea/source bank for real questions, changed decisions, artifacts, reader replies and unresolved issues. Do not invent dialogue, customer pain or founder emotions.
3. **Worthiness.** Evaluate originality, sourced specificity, reader utility, sustained explanation and relevance. Recommend skipping or resizing a thin idea. Long content must earn its length.
4. **Angle.** Research at least two materially different ways to cover the chosen issue. Present an honest hook/reader promise without guaranteed results or artificial urgency.
5. **Format.** Choose an essay, reported case, annotated demo, practical how-to or curated update. A stable masthead/signoff/recurring segment may support familiarity, but do not impose stock subheadings or formulaic paragraphs on all issues.

## During reporting and writing

- Build from actual company and external evidence, primary decisions, and audience questions, not merely a summarized weekly log.
- Plan the order of proof; show real disagreement or alternative choices and why the team chose a path. If no real tension exists, do not invent it for narrative effect.
- Explain terms for the selected reader's knowledge level. Use diagrams/screenshots/examples when permitted and genuinely useful.
- Write with an appropriate human point of view, **not** borrowed expertise or a guru persona.
- No arbitrary 400–600-word cap or artificial minimum. Format and depth serve the source material.
- Use selected copywriting research, RIOA, CUB and 'so what/prove it' as supporting tools; omit marketing pressure and outcome guarantees.

## Revision questions

- Does the opening give a relevant reader a reason to continue, without clickbait?
- Did each section advance understanding, disclose evidence, explore a meaningful decision or reveal something concrete?
- Is the tone recognizable and credible without claiming credentials the author doesn't hold?
- Could a reader confuse an internal prototype/hypothesis with a launched product or observed outcome?
- Has an independent check verified material claims from originals? Have we asked real readers/owner which passages felt confusing, unbelievable or boring?
- Did this issue make a useful *relationship promise* that the publication can realistically keep?

## Optional tactics, not mandates

Inbox Collective's 1/5/10 layering may suit a short curated service newsletter; it may damage the flow of a long-form personal essay. A recurring link section or question is optional. Reader replies and surveys can help editorial learning, but these don't create proof of product-market fit or guarantee growth.

## Skill evolution

Record raw owner feedback on the specific draft. Propose the smallest scoped improvement, test it on at least two distinct source-backed issue types, and promote only when owner or actual reader evidence indicates improvement. Keep this skill modest; detail and tenant examples belong in reference material and private workspaces.
